Full text: Like thin gruel in the poorhouses of yore, the United Nations' annual climate negotiations serve up a meager meal year after year, barely enough for participants to sustain themselves until the next round.
Last month's 29th Conference of the Parties to the UN climate treaty — COP29 — continued that tradition in Azerbaijan, dishing up a hedged pledge of $300 billion a year from a hodgepodge of sources by 2035, against a goal of $1.3 trillion.
However, by looking hard enough it is possible to find a bit more nourishment — especially from a coalition of developing and developed countries that launched an effort to accelerate action on climate change. Their initiative passed almost unnoticed but could prove one of the more significant developments of this COP.
More than 30 countries, responsible for about a third of the global gross domestic product, announced that they would adopt much firmer and faster measures to achieve net zero emissions and give the planet a fighting chance to keep within the internationally agreed 1.5 degrees Celsius guardrail against catastrophic climate change.
The announcement comes as global climate pollution continues to increase, temperature records are being broken by frightening margins, and the world speeds toward a dead man's curve where we'll crash through the 1.5 degrees Celsius guardrail. We have already reached that temperature temporarily, but as we continue to emit greenhouse gases it will be locked in.
Last month a group of scientists warned that the world is “stepping into a critical and unpredictable new phase of the climate crisis," putting us “on the brink of an irreversible climate disaster." They added that five tipping points could plunge us into a more hostile climate will be triggered at 1.5 degrees Celsius — including the loss of the Greenland Ice Sheet and the transformation of the Amazon forest from a storehouse of carbon into a source of carbon dioxide pollution.
Yet the governments in the new coalition are convinced that the world can still avoid such disasters. They have drawn inspiration from a groundbreaking report by the research company Rhodium Group, which concluded that, with ambitious but realistic measures, the world could end this century below the danger level.
The Rhodium Climate Outlook 2024 said that the world is now heading for a global average temperature rise of a scorching 2.7 degrees Celsius above preindustrial levels by 2100. That drops to a 2.6 degrees Celsius rise if the world meets its emission reduction targets for 2030 and continues at the same pace.
But the picture changes dramatically if countries that have committed to reaching net zero by mid-century both meet their 2030 targets and then accelerate action to follow an unwavering path to that goal. Then the best estimate by Rhodium is a rise of just 1.8 degrees Celsius at century's end.
In addition, if those nations that have not yet committed to net zero for all greenhouse gases nevertheless achieve it by 2070, and countries now only reducing carbon dioxide emissions expand their effort to slash methane and other climate pollutants, the world could limit warming to 1.4 degrees Celsius by 2100, albeit temporarily overshooting 1.5 degrees Celsius on the way.
That offers rare hope at a time when many experts are writing off any prospect of remaining behind the internationally agreed guardrail, with all the dangers that implies. It is vital to know that there is still a path to victory that can keep us relatively safe from climate extremes.
Among the new coalition, the United Kingdom, Brazil, and the United Arab Emirates — all among the top 30 CO2 emitters — announced new targets for 2035 consistent with stopping the world from exceeding the danger level. The EU's 27 countries — plus Canada, Chile, Norway, Georgia, and Switzerland — promised to follow suit. Mexico also joined, having previously been the only one of the G20 group of the biggest economies not to have embraced net zero.
These commitments are critical as next year all countries must produce new targets for 2035 in the run up to COP30, chaired by Brazil. Kate Larsen, lead author of the Rhodium report, said that reducing emissions of methane and other short-lived climate pollutants is important for keeping below 1.5 degrees Celsius. Indeed, studies show that cutting these emissions alongside CO2 can avoid four times as much warming by 2050 and would cut the rate of global warming in half.
Just cutting methane emissions by 45 percent below 2020 levels would shave 0.3 degrees Celsius off future warming as early as the 2040s, making avoiding tipping points much more likely.
While a few countries are beginning to take action, methane levels in the atmosphere are breaking new records. As Barbados Prime Minister Mia Mottley told the COP, a global agreement to slash methane emissions is essential for a relatively safe climate. The successful Montreal Protocol can provide the model. We still might win the battle for a safe climate, if Mottley can rally other leaders to her vision.
Durwood Zaelke is president of the Institute for Governance & Sustainable Development in Washington, D.C., and Paris, and adjunct professor at the University of California, Santa Barbara's Bren School of Environmental Science & Management. Maxime Beaugrand is director of the Paris office of the Institute for Governance & Sustainable Development.
